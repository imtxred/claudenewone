I've sold wheelchairs for twenty years. Last year, for the first time, four of them came back to me — practically new, with the receipts.

I didn't send them back to the warehouse. They're still in my stockroom to this day, lined up in a row, covered with plastic. I can't sell them. Every time I open that door, I look at them and think the same thing.

My name is Gary Whitaker. I'm fifty-two years old. I own a medical equipment store in Asheville, North Carolina — wheelchairs, walkers, crutches, orthopedic mattresses, bath lifts. Twenty years in this business. I know every supplier in the country by name.

And I know one thing about my customers that nobody is going to say out loud.

A wheelchair is a product you sell once.

Not because it lasts forever. Because the person who bought it never comes back. He comes back later for a pressure-relief cushion. Then for an anti-bedsore mattress. Then his daughter comes in and asks whether we take used equipment.

In twenty years I've sold more than two thousand wheelchairs. And not one of them ever came back to me because it wasn't needed anymore.

Until last year.

The first one came back in February. Darlene Hutchins, seventy-one, from Greenville, South Carolina. We sold her that chair in May two years earlier — I remember it, because her son took the most expensive model, the one with the lightweight frame, and haggled with me for a long time.

In February he called and said: "We want to return the chair."

I figured his mother had passed. That's the most common conversation in my line of work, and I know how it goes. I said: "My condolences."

He said: "No, no. Mom's walking. She told me to get that thing out of the house, and I can't just throw out something that cost twelve hundred dollars."

I wrote it down. I hung up. And I sat there for half a minute staring at the wall.

The second one came back in April. Roy Tillman, sixty-six, from Harlan, Kentucky. Former coal miner. His kids had chipped in and bought the chair together — I remember the invoice.

He came in alone. Driving himself. He got out of the truck, opened the tailgate, and lifted the folded chair out with one hand.

I stood in the doorway of my store and watched a man who two years earlier couldn't get himself into that chair carry it like a suitcase.

He said: "I don't need it. Give it to somebody who needs it more."

I asked: "What did you do?"

He gave me a strange look. Went quiet. Then he said: "I went to see a man. Outside Asheville. An old fellow, works with herbs."

And that was it. He didn't say another word. Got in his truck and drove off.

The third one came back in July. Wilma Prescott, seventy-eight, from Macon, Georgia. Her daughter-in-law brought it in. The chair was almost new — bought in January, used for six months.

The daughter-in-law set the receipt on the counter and said: "My mother-in-law told me to bring it back. She says if it stays in the house she'll end up sitting in it again, and she doesn't want that."

I asked her too: "What helped?"

She said: "She was rubbing something on her lower back. Some kind of gel. A neighbor brought it to her. She says it comes from some old man outside Asheville who treats people with herbs."

Second time. The same old man.

The fourth one came back in October. Curtis Vaughn, fifty-nine, from Roanoke, Virginia. Still young by our standards — herniated disc, pinched nerve, a year and a half in the chair.

He came in alone too. And he was the only one who spoke first.

He said: "Listen, this is your business. You're the one who ought to know. Why doesn't anybody tell us about this?"

I asked: "About what?"

He said: "About the fact that a wheelchair isn't a sentence. My orthopedic surgeon told me, 'Get used to it, from here on out it only gets worse.' And I got used to it. A year and a half I got used to it. And then my cousin handed me a little jar of gel and the address of a place outside Asheville."

I stood behind my counter, the counter I've stood behind for twenty years, and I said nothing.

Four wheelchairs. Four people from four different places. Not one of them knows the others. And all four named the same spot.

I didn't sleep that night. I opened up twenty years of sales records and started running the numbers.

Two thousand one hundred forty-eight wheelchairs. The average service life — I know that figure by heart, because I need it to plan my orders.

Three years and four months. That's the average time from the day the chair is sold to the day the family stops buying supplies from me.

Three years and four months.

For twenty years I built my business on that number. And only that night, at four in the morning, staring at the spreadsheet, did I understand for the first time exactly what I'd been counting for twenty years.

The next week I drove out to that place.

I won't name the town. I'll only say it's less than an hour from Asheville, and then another twenty minutes up a broken road that washes out every spring.

The house is wood. A yard. A lean-to with herbs drying under it — in bundles, hung upside down, dozens of bundles. A smell that hits you at the gate.

Grandpa Walter.

He's a hundred and two years old.

I didn't believe it when they told me. I don't believe in anything, generally — twenty years selling equipment to people nothing else has helped. My skepticism is professional.

He came out to meet me himself. Opened the gate himself. He walked without a cane.

I sell canes. I know how a man walks at seventy, at eighty, at ninety. From the way somebody walks I can tell you what he'll need in six months — a walker or a chair. That's my job.

He walked like a man of sixty.

We sat down in the yard. He asked me who I was. I told him the truth: "I sell wheelchairs. Four of them came back to me. And all four people named you."

He laughed. Quietly, with no triumph in it at all.

He said: "So I'm doing bad business for you."

I said: "Bad business."

And we both sat there quietly.

Then he asked: "And what's wrong with your own back?"

I hadn't told him about my back. I don't tell anybody about it.

Twenty years I've been unloading wheelchairs. One chair — thirty to fifty pounds. The truck comes every two weeks. Thirty, forty pieces, and I don't keep a man to help, because a man to help is another paycheck.

My back started hurting at forty-three. Around forty-eight I stopped unloading in the evenings — only in the mornings, before it seizes up. Last year, for the first time in my life, I couldn't lift my own orthopedic pillow up off the floor. The very one I sell for ninety dollars while I tell customers how much it helps.

Twenty years I've made my living off the health of people's backs. And I hide it from my wife that I sleep on my side with a pillow between my knees, because otherwise I don't fall asleep.

He knew none of that. He just asked.

I said: "Lower back. Long time now."

He said: "Show me how you sit down."

I sat down on the bench. He watched. Then he said: "You sit down on your right side. You're protecting the left. So that's where it is." And he pointed his finger at exactly the spot that not one of the four orthopedic surgeons I've been to ever put a finger on.

He went into the house and brought out a little jar. White, no label. Just something written on it by hand.

He said: "Rub it in morning and night. Ten days. If it doesn't help, forget you were ever here. But give me ten days."

I took it. Not because I believed him. Because I felt awkward walking out empty-handed after the man had given me a whole hour.

The first week, nothing.

That is — not quite nothing. On the fourth morning I didn't reach for something to hold onto while I got out of bed. I only noticed it that evening, going back over the day.

On the seventh I unloaded half a truck and never sat down on a crate to catch my breath.

On the tenth day I bent over and tied my shoes. Standing up. With both hands. Not up on my knee, not sitting on a stool, not from the side.

I stood in the stockroom of my own store, between the boxes of walkers, and my hands were shaking. I'm fifty-two years old. For four years I've been tying my shoes sitting down.

I called him that same evening.

He heard me out and said: "Good. Keep going two months. After that you won't need it anymore."

I asked: "Mr. Walter. Do you understand what you're doing to my business?"

He said: "I understand. Do you understand what you're doing with yours?"

I couldn't find anything to say to that.

Then he told me about his son. Daniel — he's not a doctor, he grew up among those herbs and scales, he's known the recipe since he was a boy. For years he'd been working on his father to keep the formula from staying shut up in one house. He found a laboratory. He found herb suppliers. He got production going so the gel could reach a person at home, instead of the person having to reach the holler.

Grandpa Walter said: "I had a six-month waiting list. People weren't living long enough to get to the front of it. In the end I agreed."

I drove back to Asheville and didn't open the store for three days.

I sat and I calculated. Not revenue — I was counting people.

Two thousand one hundred forty-eight wheelchairs in twenty years. If even one in ten of them never had to sit down in it at all — that's two hundred and fifteen people.

Two hundred and fifteen people I personally, with my own hands, carried out of my store the thing they'd go on to sit in for the rest of their lives. With a smile. With a warranty. With a thorough consultation on choosing the right model.

A month later a rep from my main supplier came to see me. We've worked together fifteen years.

He said: "Gary, what's going on with your orders? You're down thirty percent."

I told him straight: "I stopped talking people into it. Now the first thing I ask is whether they've tried everything."

He looked at me and said a sentence I wrote down word for word that same evening, because I was afraid nobody would believe me later.

He said: "Gary, our industry doesn't grow off the people who get better."

I asked: "So who does it grow off of?"

He answered: "The ones who've made their peace with it. Your job is to not get in the way of them making their peace."

He offered to raise my bonus to eighteen percent if I got back to my old volume. Eighteen instead of eleven. That's about forty thousand dollars a year.

I said: "No."

He gathered up his paperwork, stood, and said at the door: "We'll find somebody else. There are plenty."

And that is the most honest thing I've heard in twenty years in this business.

Doctors come into my store. Not as doctors — as customers.

In November an orthopedic surgeon came in. From a good hospital here in town, I've known him ten years or so, he sends me patients.

He was buying a chair for his mother. Seventy-nine years old, arthritis, the hip.

I asked him straight out: "Doc, why don't you operate on her? You do the surgery."

He was picking out a model, not lifting his head. Then he said: "Because I know how those operations end at seventy-nine."

I put the catalog away. I gave him the address.

He wrote it down. He didn't argue. He didn't say one word about quackery, placebo, or the power of suggestion — and I was braced to hear all three.

In February he stopped by again. He didn't take the chair.

And now the reason I'm writing any of this.

My mother. Eleanor. Seventy-six years old. She lives in Hickory, North Carolina, alone, in a house with a high front stoop.

For three years she told me on the phone that everything was "just fine, honey."

For three years I had a store in Asheville full of equipment that could have helped her. I brought her walkers — she wouldn't touch them. I brought her grab bars for the bathroom — she wouldn't let anybody screw them into the wall.

Last spring I drove out without calling first and found her sitting on the front steps. She'd made it up to the third one and sat down to catch her breath. She didn't know how long she'd been sitting there.

Twenty years I've sold equipment for people like my mother. And I couldn't get my own mother to use any of it, because to her it meant admitting it was all over.

In June I brought her a little jar. Not a chair, not a walker, not grab bars. A jar.

The first week I rubbed it on her myself, morning and night, because she said she couldn't reach her lower back. After that she started doing it on her own.

In September she called me one evening and told me she'd walked out to the cemetery to see my father. On foot. That's a mile and a half from the house, uphill the whole way.

I listened to her and stayed quiet on the phone, because I couldn't talk.

I'm not a doctor. I'm not an herbalist. I'm not a scientist. I'm a wheelchair salesman whose year fell apart.

I don't know how that formula works at the cellular level. They explained it to me — something about how the substances go through the skin straight into the joint, instead of through the stomach and the liver the way pills do. I haven't checked it. I don't have a laboratory.

But I have a stockroom. And in it stand four wheelchairs, covered with plastic.

That's my laboratory. It's the only one I've got.

If you're reading this, it means you or your parents have pain in the knees, the hips, the back.

Maybe they've already told you: "At your age that's normal." Maybe they've already written you a prescription for a cream from the pharmacy that takes the pain off for four hours. Maybe they've already quoted you the price of the surgery — and you've worked out how many months of Social Security that comes to.

Or maybe you're standing in my store right now, picking out a model.

I want you to know: the man behind the counter is not going to talk you out of it. He has a month to close. He's got a quota coming down on him. He gets his eleven percent, and if he really works at it — eighteen.

For twenty years I was that man.

Don't wait until the cartilage is completely gone. Don't wait until the pinched nerve dies — a disc can still come back, a nerve can't, that's what they explained to me out there outside Asheville and it's the one thing I remembered word for word.

Don't wait until your kids start talking to each other about a nursing home.

Below this there's a blue button — "Learn More." That's the official article from Grandpa Walter: what's in it, how it works, accounts from people who got back on their feet. Read it carefully.

Delivered by courier right to your door, and you pay when it arrives. Three or four days, anywhere in the country.

Forward this to your mother. To your father. To your husband. To your wife. To anybody who gets up in the morning groaning and tells you everything's fine.

I have sold two thousand one hundred forty-eight wheelchairs.

I'd like every one of them back.

Sincerely,
Gary Whitaker, 52, Asheville, North Carolina
