I cancelled my own operation nine hours before the anaesthetic. It was me they were going to open up — a neurosurgeon with thirty years behind him.

The refusal form is sitting in my desk drawer. I wrote it myself, by hand, at half eleven at night, in the doctors' room where I've spent twenty-two years. In my own handwriting, the one I've signed operation notes with for thirty years.

My name is Eamon Mulcahy. I'm fifty-eight. I'm a neurosurgeon. Thirty years operating on spines in Dublin.

I never counted my operations until I sat down to write this. I counted them. More than three thousand eight hundred.

Three thousand eight hundred people I opened up.

And there's one thing I know about the spine that I tell patients every day of the week. Thirty years I've been telling them.

There's no going back.

A destroyed disc doesn't rebuild itself. Worn cartilage doesn't grow back. All medicine can do is take away what's pressing on the nerve and fix down what's moving. With metal. With screws. With cages.

I believed that. That's how I was taught here, and after that on fellowship in Melbourne. And I passed it on — to two generations of young doctors operating in this country today.

And then I needed an operation myself.

I'll tell it in order, because otherwise it reads like a madman's confession.

A surgeon has an occupational illness the brochures never mention. We stand. A spinal operation is four hours, six, sometimes eight, in the one position, bent forward, in a lead apron, under the X-ray arm.

A lead apron weighs seven kilos. In thirty years I've spent about twenty thousand hours inside one.

It started hurting me at fifty-one. First after the long operations. Then after all of them. Then for no reason at all.

I made the diagnosis myself, in three minutes, looking at my own scan. Degenerative lumbar canal stenosis with a slip. Textbook. I operate on those with my eyes shut.

I told nobody.

For four years I operated on painkillers. A colleague in anaesthetics gave me blocks in the changing room — as a favour, with nothing written in the chart, because a note in the chart would have meant being taken off the theatre list.

Then the blocks stopped lasting a whole operation. They'd hold for four hours.

Last March, for the first time in my life, I handed an operation over to my registrar halfway through. I stood there gripping the edge of the table, telling him where to cut, because I couldn't stay standing any longer.

He's a great lad. He said nothing about it, not then and not since.

A week later I put myself down for an operation. With my colleague, Mr Barry Tobin. We were in college together. The best pair of hands in the department after my own.

The date was the fourteenth of May.

And now the thing that broke it all.

I keep a folder. Nineteen years I've kept it, and two people in the hospital knew about it.

It's the folder of the ones who said no. Patients I offered an operation to, and who refused.

I didn't start it out of pity. I started it out of spite — as a young surgeon I wanted to gather proof that refusing an operation ends badly. I was going to write a paper on it.

For nineteen years I wrote surnames into it and then followed what became of them. Some came back after a year, after two, worse again. Some never came back at all, and I'd rule a line through them once I heard it from the family.

I never wrote the paper.

Because from about two thousand and nineteen on, entries started appearing in that folder that fitted into no paper at all.

Jimmy Prendergast. Sixty-nine, Ballina. Stenosis, two herniations, refused in two thousand and twenty-two.

He came back for review fourteen months later. On his own. On foot, no stick, up the stairs to the first floor, because the lift was out that day.

I looked at his scans for five minutes. Then I asked him what he'd done.

He said: 'I went to a man. A village out in Wicklow. An old fella, does something with herbs.'

I wrote it into the folder and put a question mark beside it.

Josie. Seventy-four, Ennis. A bad case, I offered her a three-level fusion. She refused in May, and the August after that she sent me a letter and a photograph through her daughter.

In the photograph she's standing in the garden with a hoe.

The daughter passed on the message: 'Mammy said not to be cross with her.'

Christy. Sixty-three, Clonmel. Refused twice — in twenty-one and in twenty-three. The second time he said a line to me that I took as cheek then and that I think about every week now.

He said: 'Doctor, you're a good man. But you're offering me the only thing you know how to do.'

By last spring there were twenty-six entries like that in the folder. Twenty-six people who turned down my knife and were walking better a year or two later than they had been before.

Twenty-six — out of more than three hundred who refused across nineteen years. That's no majority. I know how numbers like that are read.

But eleven of those twenty-six, when I asked them, named the same thing. A village out in Wicklow. An old man. Herbs.

Eleven people from different towns, none of whom know each other.

On the thirteenth of May, the night before my own operation, I didn't sleep. At two in the morning I got up, went into the study and took out that folder.

I read it until four. Then I sat there looking at my hands.

At half eleven — or rather, already the fourteenth of May, nine hours before the anaesthetic — I wrote the refusal. I brought it down to the nurse on duty. She looked at me and never said a word.

Barry rang me in the morning. He didn't shout. He only asked: 'Do you understand what you're doing?'

I said: 'No.'

That was an honest answer.

Four days later I drove out to that village.

I won't name it. Less than an hour from Dublin, and then a road my car grounded itself on twice.

A wooden house. A yard. A lean-to with bunches of dried herbs hanging under it — dozens of them, heads down. A smell that hits you at the gate.

And a bench by the wall, with a man sitting on it.

I knew him straight away.

Noel Hanrahan. Mullingar. I operated on him twice — in two thousand and eighteen and two thousand and twenty. The second was a revision, because he was worse after the first.

After the second he was worse again.

I remember him sitting across from me in the clinic asking what happens now. I told him what you tell people in that situation: 'From here — painkillers and rest.'

I hadn't seen him in four years.

He was sitting on the bench in the old man's yard and he shook my hand. Standing. He'd got up when he saw me.

He said: 'Doctor. You're here too?'

I couldn't answer him. I stood in the middle of another man's yard, a neurosurgeon with thirty years behind him, and I couldn't get a word out to a man I'd cut open twice myself.

He said: 'Ah, don't be worrying about it. You did what you knew how to do.'

They're the very words Christy said to me. A different man, a different town, the same sentence.

Séamus Kavanagh came out to me himself.

He's a hundred and two.

I didn't believe it until I saw him walk. I've a professional deformity: from the way a person walks I can tell you what I'll see on their scan. It's enough for me to follow them with my eyes from the door to the couch.

He opened the gate himself and crossed the yard without a stick.

Thirty years I've been watching people of seventy walk, of eighty, of ninety. I know what it looks like.

He walked like a man of sixty.

We sat down in the yard. He asked me who I was. I told him the truth: 'I'm a neurosurgeon. There are eleven surnames in my folder, and every one of them named you.'

He laughed. Quietly, with no triumph in it at all.

Then he looked at me and said: 'You're standing on the left leg.'

I was standing on the left leg. I've been standing on it four years, because the right one shoots into my backside the moment I put weight through it. Not one colleague of mine, Barry included, the man who was going to operate on me, had noticed it — they were looking at the scans.

He said: 'Show me how you sit down.'

I sat down. He watched. Then he put his finger into my lower back — into the exact segment marked on my MRI.

Thirty years I've been teaching students that the clinical examination matters more than the scan. I stopped doing it myself somewhere around my fifteenth year in practice.

He went into the house and came out with a small jar of capsules. White, no label. Only writing by hand.

He said: 'Take one morning and night. Ten days. If it doesn't help — forget you were ever here. But give me the ten days.'

I took it. Me, who has spent thirty years explaining to patients that cartilage has no blood supply and so whatever you swallow never reaches it, took a jar with no ingredients, no certificate and not one study behind it.

I took it because four days earlier I'd cancelled my own operation and had nothing left to lose.

The first week, nothing.

That is — not quite nothing. On the fourth morning I got out of bed without rolling onto my side first. I simply sat up and stood. I only noticed it that evening, going back over the day.

On the seventh I stood a whole operation. Three hours and forty minutes. No block.

On the tenth day I came out of theatre, took off the apron, hung it in the locker — and realised I wasn't walking over to the sink to lean my hands on it, the way I'd done for four years straight.

I stood on my own in the anaesthetic room and couldn't move off the spot.

I rang him that same evening.

He heard me out and said: 'Good. Keep going two months. After that you won't be needing it.'

I asked him: 'Do you realise that if this works, I've been doing the wrong thing for thirty years?'

He was quiet. Then he said: 'You did what you were taught. That's not the same thing.'

Then he told me about his son. Declan is no doctor. He grew up among those herbs and scales, he's known the recipe since he was a child. For years he argued with his father that the recipe shouldn't stay in the one village. In the end he found a laboratory and people who helped turn it into a proper product in a proper box. So that the jar travels to the person, instead of the person travelling to the village.

The old man didn't agree straight away. He had a waiting list six months long, and he said only one thing to me: 'People weren't living long enough to reach the top of it.'

Six weeks later the rep from the company that supplies us the fixation systems came into my office. We've dealt with each other eleven years.

He asked was everything all right: the department's numbers for instrumented procedures were down.

I told him straight: 'I've started talking more people out of it.'

He smiled and said that was a matter of clinical conscience, of course, and then reminded me about the congress in Lisbon in the autumn. Registration, flights, hotel — as always, on them. Plus a lecture, paid separately.

And he added: 'You understand, Professor, our budgets are worked out on volume.'

I won't put a threat on him that wasn't there. He was polite. He only said out loud how the thing works.

I didn't go to the congress.

Two colleagues have stopped talking to me. One of them runs the department next door, a man I drank tea with in the same room for twenty years.

But in November our consultant anaesthetist called in to me. The same man who gave me blocks in the changing room for four years.

He closed the door and asked quietly: 'Eamon. They say you have an address.'

I asked: 'For a patient?'

He said: 'For my mother.'

She's eighty-one. Osteoarthritis, hip. He's an anaesthetist with twenty-five years behind him and knows more about operations than most surgeons.

I asked him: 'And why aren't they operating on her?'

He answered: 'Because I've been standing at the head of the table twenty-five years and I've seen how that ends at eighty-one.'

I gave him the address.

And now the reason I'm writing any of this down.

My mother. Sheila. Eighty-three. She lives in Birr, in the house I grew up in.

For four years she told me on the phone that everything was grand, son.

For four years she had a son who was a neurosurgeon, a professor, a head of department. And for four years she never told me she'd stopped going to Mass, because she couldn't manage the standing and the kneeling any more.

I heard it from the woman next door.

In July I brought her a jar. Not a referral, not an appointment with a colleague, not a place on a theatre list — a jar.

The first week I gave her the capsules myself, morning and night, because she said she'd only forget them.

At Christmas she was at Mass. She stood and she knelt along with everyone else. She rang me that evening and told me in passing, as if it meant nothing.

I listened and said nothing into the phone, because I couldn't speak.

I won't draw conclusions I've no right to draw. I ran no randomised trial. I have no control group. All I have is a folder I've kept for nineteen years and my own back.

But for thirty years I said the word 'irreversible' to people. I said it with certainty, because I was certain.

I say it less often now. First I ask: 'Have you tried everything?'

And that's the only change in my professional life I'm genuinely proud of in the last ten years.

If you're reading this, it means that you, or your parents, have pain in the knees, the hips, the back.

Maybe you've already been told: 'At your age that's normal.' Maybe you've already been given a cream from the chemist that lasts four hours. Maybe you've already been quoted the price of the operation — and you've worked out how many months of the State Pension that comes to.

And maybe you're already sitting in a corridor outside a clinic where somebody like me is going to explain to you that there's no alternative.

There is an alternative. I say that as a man who spent thirty years insisting there wasn't.

Don't wait until the cartilage is gone altogether. Don't wait until the trapped nerve dies — the disc can still come back, the nerve can't. I know that for certain, it's my own field, and it's the one thing in my speciality that hasn't changed.

Don't wait until your children start talking to each other about a nursing home.

Under this text there's a blue button — 'Find out more'. That's where Séamus Kavanagh's official article is: what's in it, how it works, accounts from people who got back on their feet. Read it carefully.

The courier delivers in three to four days, you pay on receipt, anywhere in Ireland.

Send this on to your mother. To your father. To your husband. To your wife. To anyone who gets up in the morning with a groan and tells you everything's grand.

I've done more than three thousand eight hundred operations.

And the most correct of the lot turned out to be the one I cancelled nine hours before the anaesthetic.

With respect,
Mr Eamon Mulcahy, neurosurgeon, 58, Dublin
