For twenty years I have been selling wheelchairs. Last year, for the first time, four of them were brought back to me — nearly new, with the receipts.

I did not send them back to the warehouse. To this day they are standing in my store room, lined up in a row, covered over with plastic. I cannot bring myself to sell them. Every time I open that door I look at them and think the same thing.

My name is Niall Doyle. I am fifty-two years of age. I have a shop selling mobility equipment in Dublin — wheelchairs, walkers, crutches, orthopaedic mattresses, bath lifts. Twenty years in this trade. I know every supplier in the country by name.

And there is one thing I know about my customers that nobody will say out loud to you.

A wheelchair is a thing you sell once.

Not because it lasts forever. But because the person who bought it never comes back. He comes back later for a pressure-relief cushion. Then for a pressure-relief mattress. Then his daughter comes in and asks whether we take second-hand equipment.

In twenty years I have sold more than two thousand wheelchairs. And not a single one of them ever came back to me because it was no longer needed.

Until last year.

The first was brought in in February. Rosaleen Hanley, seventy-one, from Mullingar. We had sold her the chair two years earlier in May — I remember it, because her son took the dearest one, with the lightweight frame, and haggled over it for ages.

In February he rang and said: “We want to bring the wheelchair back.”

I assumed his mother had died. That is the most common conversation in my line of work and I know how it goes. I said: “I am very sorry for your loss.”

He answered: “No, no. Mam is walking. She told me to get that thing out of the house, and I cannot put a nineteen-hundred-euro item out with the rubbish.”

I made a note of it. I hung up. And for half a minute I sat there staring at the wall.

The second was brought in in April. Jimmy Moran, sixty-six, from Silvermines in County Tipperary. A former miner. His children had clubbed together to buy him the chair; I remember the invoice.

He came on his own. Driving. He got out of the car, opened the boot and lifted the folded wheelchair out with one hand.

I stood in the door of my shop and watched a man who two years earlier could not get himself into that chair unaided now carrying it like a suitcase.

He said: “I do not need it. Give it to somebody who needs it more.”

I asked: “What did you do?”

He gave me a strange sort of a look. He said nothing for a moment. Then he said: “I went to see a man. Outside Dublin. An old fellow, he does something with herbs.”

And that was that. He said nothing more. He got into the car and drove off.

The third was brought in in July. Maureen Kiely, seventy-eight, from Ennis. Her daughter-in-law brought it in. The chair was nearly new — bought in January, used for half a year.

The daughter-in-law put the receipt down on the counter and said: “My mother-in-law gave orders to bring it back. She says if it stays in the house she will end up sitting into it again, and she does not want that.”

I asked her the same question: “And what helped?”

She said: “She was taking something. Some sort of capsules. A neighbour brought them in to her. She says they are from some old man outside Dublin who heals with herbs.”

The second time. The same old man.

The fourth was brought in in October. Frank Delaney, fifty-nine, from Portlaoise. Young still, by the standards of our trade — a herniated disc, a trapped nerve, a year and a half in the chair.

He came on his own as well. And he was the only one who spoke first.

He said: “Listen, you are the man who sells these things. You must know. Why does nobody tell us about this?”

I asked: “About what?”

He said: “About the fact that a wheelchair is not a sentence. My consultant said to me: ‘Get used to it, from here on it only gets worse.’ And I got used to it. A year and a half I spent getting used to it. And then my cousin gave me a little tub of capsules and the address of a village outside Dublin.”

I stood behind my own counter, the counter I have stood behind for twenty years, and I said nothing.

Four wheelchairs. Four people from four different counties. Not one of them knows the others. And all four of them named the same place.

That night I did not sleep. I opened the sales database going back twenty years and started doing sums.

Two thousand one hundred and forty-eight wheelchairs. The average “service life” — I know that figure off by heart, because I need it for planning my orders.

Three years and four months. That is how long it takes on average from the sale of the chair to the moment the family stops buying consumables from me.

Three years and four months.

For twenty years I built my business on that figure. And only that night, at four in the morning, with my eyes fixed on that table, did I understand for the first time what exactly I had been counting for twenty years.

The following week I drove out to that village.

I will not name the village. I will only say that it is less than an hour from Dublin, and then another twenty minutes along a broken road that washes out in the spring.

The house is timber. A yard. A lean-to with herbs drying under it — in bunches, hung heads down, dozens of bunches. A smell that hits you the moment you reach the gate.

Old Séamus.

He is a hundred and two.

I did not believe it when they told me. I do not believe in anything at all — for twenty years I have been selling equipment to people whom everything else has already failed. My scepticism is a professional condition.

He came out to me himself. He opened the gate himself. He walked without a stick.

I sell walking sticks. I know how a man of seventy walks, of eighty, of ninety. From the way a person walks I can tell you what they will need in six months' time — a walker or a chair. That is my job.

He walked like a man of sixty.

We sat down in the yard. He asked me who I was. I told him the truth: “I sell wheelchairs. Four of them have been brought back to me. And all four people named you.”

He laughed. Quietly, without a trace of triumph.

He said: “So I am doing badly by you.”

I said: “Badly.”

The two of us sat there in silence.

Then he asked: “And what is wrong with your own back?”

I had not told him anything about my back. I do not tell anybody about it.

For twenty years I have been unloading wheelchairs. One chair — anything from fourteen to twenty-three kilos. The lorry comes once a fortnight. Thirty or forty items, and I do not keep a man on to do the lifting, because a man to do the lifting is another wage.

My back started at forty-three. By about forty-eight I stopped unloading in the evenings — mornings only, while it had not yet “seized”. Last year, for the first time in my life, I could not lift my own orthopaedic cushion up off the floor. The very cushion I sell for seventy-nine euro and tell my customers how much it helps.

For twenty years I have been trading in the health of people's backs. And I hide it from my wife that I sleep on my side with a pillow between my knees, because otherwise I do not get to sleep at all.

He knew none of that. He simply asked.

I said: “The lower back. A long time now.”

He said: “Show me how you sit down.”

I sat down on the bench. He watched. Then he said: “You sit down on the right side. You are sparing the left. So that is where it is.” And he put his finger on the exact spot that not one of the four consultants I had been to had ever put a finger on.

He went into the house and came out with a small tub. White, no label. Just something written on it by hand.

He said: “Take one capsule morning and evening. Ten days. If it does not help, forget you were ever here. But give me ten days.”

I took it. Not because I believed in it. But because I was embarrassed to walk away empty-handed after the man had given me a full hour of his time.

The first week, nothing.

That is to say — not quite nothing. On the fourth morning I did not reach out a hand for something to lean on as I got out of the bed. I only noticed it that evening, going back over the day in my head.

On the seventh I unloaded half a lorry and did not sit down on a crate to catch my breath.

On the tenth day I bent down and tied my own laces. Standing up. With both hands. Not through the knee, not on a stool, not sideways.

I stood in the store room of my own shop, between the boxes of walkers, and my hands were shaking. I am fifty-two years of age. For four years I have been tying my laces sitting down.

I rang him that same evening.

He heard me out and said: “Good. Keep going for two months. After that you will not need it any more.”

I asked: “Séamus. Do you understand what you are doing to my business?”

He said: “I do. And do you understand what you are doing to yours?”

I could not find an answer to that.

Then he told me about his son. Declan — he is not a doctor, he grew up among those herbs and weighing scales, he has known the recipe since he was a child. For years he tried to convince his father that the formulation should not stay in one village. He found a laboratory. He found suppliers for the herbs. He got the production going, so that the tub could come to the person's house instead of the person having to come to the village.

Old Séamus said: “I had a six-month waiting list. People were not living long enough to reach the top of it. In the end I agreed.”

I drove back to Dublin and did not open the shop for three days.

I sat there doing sums. Not turnover — I was counting people.

Two thousand one hundred and forty-eight wheelchairs in twenty years. If even one in ten of them need never have sat into one at all — that is two hundred and fifteen people.

Two hundred and fifteen people for whom I personally, with my own two hands, carried out of the shop the thing they then sat into for the rest of their lives. With a smile. With a warranty. With a detailed consultation on choosing the right model.

A month later the representative of my main supplier came in to me. We have been working together for fifteen years.

He said: “Niall, what is going on with your orders? You are down thirty per cent.”

I told him honestly: “I have stopped talking people into it. Now I ask first whether they have tried everything.”

He looked at me and said a sentence that I wrote down word for word that same evening, because I was afraid nobody would believe me afterwards.

He said: “Niall, our trade does not grow on the people who get better.”

I asked: “What does it grow on, then?”

He answered: “On the ones who have resigned themselves to it. Your job is not to get in the way of them resigning themselves to it.”

He offered to raise my bonus to eighteen per cent if I went back to my previous volumes. Eighteen instead of eleven. That is around forty thousand euro a year.

I said: “No.”

He gathered up his papers, stood up and said at the door: “We will find somebody else. There are plenty of them.”

And that is the most honest thing I have heard in twenty years in this trade.

Doctors come in to me. Not as doctors — as customers.

In November an orthopaedic consultant came in. From a good Dublin hospital, I have known him a good ten years, he sends me patients.

He was buying a wheelchair for his mother. Seventy-nine, osteoarthritis, the hip.

I asked him straight out: “Doctor, why do you not operate on her? You do operate.”

He was picking out a model without lifting his head. Then he said: “Because I know how operations like that end at seventy-nine.”

I put the catalogue away. I gave him the address of the village.

He wrote it down. He did not argue. He did not say one word about quackery, placebo or the power of suggestion — and I had braced myself to hear all three.

In February he called in again. He did not take the wheelchair.

And now the thing I am writing all of this for in the first place.

My mother. Teresa. Seventy-six. She lives in Navan, on her own, in a house with a steep flight of stairs.

For three years she told me on the phone that with her “everything is grand, son”.

For three years I had a shop in Dublin full of equipment that could have helped her. I brought her walkers — she would not touch them. I brought her grab rails for the bathroom — she would not let anyone screw them to the wall.

Last spring I called out without warning and found her sitting on the stairs. She had got as far as the third step and had sat down to catch her breath. She did not know how long she had been sitting there.

For twenty years I have been selling equipment for people like my mother. And I could not get my own mother to use it, because for her that meant admitting that it was all over.

In June I brought her a tub. Not a wheelchair, not a walker, not grab rails. A tub.

The first week I gave her the capsules myself, morning and evening, because otherwise she would forget. After that she started taking them herself.

In September she rang me in the evening and said she had been up to the graveyard to my father. On foot. That is a mile and a half from the house, and it is uphill the whole way.

I listened to her and said nothing into the phone, because I could not speak.

I am not a doctor. I am not a herbalist. I am not a scientist. I am a wheelchair dealer whose year went wrong.

I do not know how that formula works at cell level. They explained it to me — something about nanoparticles that pass through the stomach intact, only release in the small intestine and travel through the bloodstream straight into the joint, to places creams and ointments never reach. I have not verified it. I do not have a laboratory.

But I have a store room. And in it stand four wheelchairs, covered over with plastic.

That is my laboratory. I do not have another one.

If you are reading this, then it means that you yourself, or your parents, have pain in the knees, the hips, the back.

Maybe you have already been told: “At your age that is normal.” Maybe you have already been prescribed a cream from the chemist that takes the pain away for four hours. Maybe you have already been quoted the price of the operation — and you have worked out how many pensions that comes to.

And maybe you are already standing in my shop picking out a model.

I want you to know this: the man behind the counter will not talk you out of it. He has a month to close. He has targets handed down to him. He gets his eleven per cent, and if he makes an effort — eighteen.

For twenty years I was that man.

Do not wait until the cartilage is completely destroyed. Do not wait until the trapped nerve dies — the disc can still come back, the nerve cannot; that was explained to me in that village outside Dublin and it is the only thing I remembered word for word.

Do not wait until your children start talking among themselves about a nursing home.

Forward this on to your mother. To your father. To your husband. To your wife. To anyone who gets up in the morning with a groan and tells you that everything is grand.

I have sold two thousand one hundred and forty-eight wheelchairs.

I would like every one of them brought back to me.

Yours sincerely,
Niall Doyle, 52, Dublin
