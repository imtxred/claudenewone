Nine hours before the anaesthetic, I cancelled my own operation. It was me they were going to open — a neurosurgeon of thirty years standing.

The refusal letter is in my drawer. I wrote it myself, by hand, at half past eleven at night, in the doctors' room where I have spent twenty-two years. In my own handwriting, the same one I have signed operation notes with for thirty years.

My name is Obiora Ezenwa. I am fifty-eight. I am a neurosurgeon. Thirty years operating on spines in Lagos.

I had never counted my operations until I sat down to write this. I have counted them. More than three thousand eight hundred.

Three thousand eight hundred people I have opened.

And there is one thing I know about the spine that I tell patients every single day. Thirty years I have been telling them.

There is no going back.

A destroyed disc does not rebuild itself. Worn cartilage does not grow again. All medicine can do is remove what is pressing on the nerve and fix down what is loose. With metal. With screws. With cages.

I believed it. That is how they taught me here, and after that on fellowship in London. And I passed it on — to two generations of young doctors who are operating in this country today.

And then I needed an operation myself.

Let me tell it in order, otherwise it will read like the confession of a madman.

A surgeon has an occupational disease that the brochures never mention. We stand. A spine operation is four hours, six, sometimes eight, in the same position, bent forward, in a lead apron, under the X-ray arm.

A lead apron weighs seven kilograms. In thirty years I have spent about twenty thousand hours inside one.

The pain started at fifty-one. First after the long cases. Then after all of them. Then for no reason at all.

I made the diagnosis myself, in three minutes, from my own scan. Degenerative lumbar canal stenosis with a slip. Textbook. I operate on such cases with my eyes closed.

I told nobody.

For four years I operated on painkillers. A colleague in anaesthesia gave me blocks in the changing room — as a favour, with nothing entered in the file, because an entry in the file would have meant being taken off the theatre list.

Then the blocks could no longer carry a whole operation. They would hold for four hours.

In March last year, for the first time in my life, I handed an operation over to my registrar halfway through. I stood there gripping the edge of the table, telling him where to cut, because I myself could no longer remain standing.

He is a fine young man. He has never said a word about it, not that day and not since.

A week later I booked myself for surgery. With my colleague, Dr Femi Akintola. We were in medical school together. The best hands in the department after mine.

The date was the fourteenth of May.

And now the thing on which it all broke.

I keep a file. Nineteen years I have kept it, and two people in the hospital knew about it.

It is the file of those who refused. Patients I offered an operation to, and who said no.

I did not start it out of pity. I started it out of stubbornness — as a young surgeon I wanted to gather proof that refusing an operation ends badly. I intended to write a paper on it.

For nineteen years I entered surnames in it and followed what became of each one. Some came back after a year, after two, worse already. Some never came back at all, and I would rule a line through them once the family told me.

I never wrote the paper.

Because from about two thousand and nineteen, entries began to appear in that file that fitted into no paper at all.

Sylvester Ndukwe. Sixty-nine years, Aba. Stenosis, two herniations. He refused in two thousand and twenty-two.

He came for review fourteen months later. Alone. On foot, no walking stick, up the staircase to the first floor, because the lift was not working that day.

I looked at his scans for five minutes. Then I asked him what he had done.

He said: 'I went to a man. A village on the outskirts of Lagos. An old baba, he does something with herbs.'

I wrote it into the file and put a question mark beside it.

Comfort. Seventy-four years, Uyo. A difficult case, I offered her a three-level fusion. She refused in May, and the August that followed she sent me a letter and a photograph through her daughter.

In the photograph she is standing in the garden with a hoe.

The daughter passed on the message by mouth: 'Mama said you should not be annoyed with her.'

Godwin. Sixty-three years, Warri. He refused twice — in twenty-one and again in twenty-three. The second time he said a sentence to me that I took as rudeness then, and that I think about every week now.

He said: 'Doctor, you are a good man. But you are offering me the only thing you know how to do.'

By last spring there were twenty-six such entries in the file. Twenty-six people who refused my knife and were walking better a year or two later than they had been before.

Twenty-six — out of more than three hundred refusals in nineteen years. That is not a majority. I know how such figures are supposed to be read.

But eleven of those twenty-six, when I asked, gave me the same answer. A village on the outskirts of Lagos. An old man. Herbs.

Eleven people from different towns, none of whom know one another.

On the thirteenth of May, the night before my own operation, I did not sleep. At two in the morning I got up, went into the study and took out that file.

I read it until four. Then I sat looking at my hands.

At half past eleven — or rather, already the fourteenth of May, nine hours before the anaesthetic — I wrote the refusal. I took it to the nurse on duty. She looked at me and did not say one word.

Femi called me in the morning. He did not shout. He asked only: 'Do you understand what you are doing?'

I said: 'No.'

That was an honest answer.

Four days later I drove out to that village.

I will not name it. Less than an hour from Lagos, and then a road on which my car scraped its belly twice.

A wooden house. A compound. A shed under which bunches of dried herbs are hanging — dozens of them, heads down. A smell that meets you at the gate.

And a bench by the fence, with a man sitting on it.

I knew him at once.

Danladi Bako. Minna. I had operated on him twice — in two thousand and eighteen and in two thousand and twenty. The second was a revision, because after the first he was worse.

After the second he was worse still.

I remember him sitting across from me in the clinic asking what next. I told him what one tells people in that situation: 'From here — painkillers and rest.'

I had not seen him in four years.

He was sitting on the bench in the old man's compound and he shook my hand. Standing. He had risen when he saw me.

He said: 'Doctor. You too are here?'

I could not answer. I stood in the middle of another man's compound, a neurosurgeon of thirty years standing, and I could not get one word out to a man I had cut open twice myself.

He said: 'Ah, do not worry yourself. You did what you knew how to do.'

Those are the very words Godwin said to me. Another man, another town, the same words exactly.

Baba Adewale came out to me himself.

He is a hundred and two.

I did not believe it until I saw him walk. I have a professional deformity: from the way a person walks I can tell you what I will see on his scan. It is enough for me to follow him with my eyes from the door to the couch.

He opened the gate himself and crossed the compound without a stick.

Thirty years I have watched how people of seventy walk, of eighty, of ninety. I know what it looks like.

He walked like a man of sixty.

We sat down in the compound. He asked me who I was. I told him the truth: 'I am a neurosurgeon. There are eleven surnames in my file, and every one of them named you.'

He laughed. Quietly, without any triumph in it.

Then he looked at me and said: 'You are standing on the left leg.'

I was standing on the left leg. I have stood on it for four years, because the right one shoots into my buttock the moment I put weight through it. Not one of my colleagues, Femi included, the man who was to operate on me, had noticed it — they were looking at the scans.

He said: 'Show me how you sit down.'

I sat down. He watched. Then he pushed his finger into my lower back — into the exact segment marked on my MRI.

Thirty years I have been teaching students that the clinical examination matters more than the scan. I myself stopped doing it somewhere in my fifteenth year of practice.

He went into the house and came out with a small bottle of capsules. White, no label. Only writing by hand.

He said: 'Take one morning and night. Ten days. If it does not help — forget that you ever came. But give me the ten days.'

I took it. I, who have spent thirty years explaining to patients that cartilage has no blood supply and that whatever you swallow therefore never reaches it, took a bottle with no composition, no certificate and not one study behind it.

I took it because four days earlier I had cancelled my own operation and had nothing left to lose.

The first week, nothing.

That is — not quite nothing. On the fourth morning I got out of bed without first rolling onto my side. I simply sat up and stood. I noticed it only in the evening, going back over the day.

On the seventh I stood through a whole operation. Three hours and forty minutes. No block.

On the tenth day I came out of theatre, took off the apron, hung it in the locker — and realised that I was not walking to the sink to lean my hands on it, the way I had done four years running.

I stood alone in the anaesthetic room and could not move from the spot.

I called him that same evening.

He heard me out and said: 'Good. Continue two months. After that you will not be needing it.'

I asked him: 'Do you realise that if this works, I have been doing the wrong thing for thirty years?'

He was silent. Then he said: 'You did what they taught you. That is not the same thing.'

Then he told me about his son. Tunde is not a doctor. He grew up among those herbs and scales, he has known the recipe since childhood. For years he pressed his father that the recipe should not remain in one village. In the end he found a laboratory and people who helped to turn it into a proper product in a proper pack. So that the bottle travels to the person, and not the person to the village.

The old man did not agree at once. He had a waiting list of six months, and he said only one thing to me: 'The people were not living long enough to reach it.'

Six weeks later the representative of the company that supplies us the fixation systems came to my office. We have worked together eleven years.

He asked whether everything was in order: the department's numbers for instrumented procedures had fallen.

I said it plainly: 'I have started talking more people out of it.'

He smiled and said that was of course a matter of medical conscience, and then reminded me about the congress in Dubai in the autumn. Registration, flight, hotel — as always, on them. Plus a lecture, paid separately.

And he added: 'Prof, you understand, our budgets are calculated on volume.'

I do not want to put on him a threat that was not there. He was polite. He only said aloud how the thing works.

I did not go to the congress.

Two colleagues no longer speak to me. One of them heads the department next door, a man with whom I drank tea in the same room for twenty years.

But in November our chief anaesthetist came to see me. The very man who had been giving me blocks in the changing room for four years.

He shut the door and asked quietly: 'Obiora. They say you have an address.'

I asked: 'For a patient?'

He said: 'For my mother.'

She is eighty-one. Osteoarthritis, hip. He is an anaesthetist of twenty-five years standing and knows more about operations than most surgeons.

I asked him: 'And why are they not operating on her?'

He answered: 'Because I have been standing at the head of the table for twenty-five years and I have seen how that ends at eighty-one.'

I gave him the address.

And now the reason I am writing all of this at all.

My mother. Regina. Eighty-three years. She lives in Awka, alone, in the house I grew up in.

For four years she told me on the phone that with her 'everything is fine, my son'.

For four years she had a son who is a neurosurgeon, a professor, a head of department. And for four years she did not tell me that she had stopped going to church, because she could no longer stand through the service.

I heard it from the woman next door.

In July I took her a bottle. Not a referral, not an appointment with a colleague, not a slot on a theatre list — a bottle.

The first week I gave her the capsules myself, morning and night, because she said she would only forget them.

At Christmas she stood through the whole service. She called me that evening and told me in passing, as though it meant nothing.

I listened and kept silent into the phone, because I could not speak.

I will not draw conclusions I have no right to draw. I ran no randomised trial. I have no control group. All I have is a file I have kept for nineteen years and my own back.

But for thirty years I used the word 'irreversible' with people. I used it with full confidence, because I was confident.

Now I say it less often. First I ask: 'Have you tried everything?'

And that is the only professional change in the last ten years that I am genuinely proud of.

If you are reading this, it means that you, or your parents, have pain in the knees, the hips, the back.

Perhaps you have already been told: 'At your age that is normal.' Perhaps you have already been given an ointment from the chemist that takes the pain away for four hours. Perhaps you have already been quoted the price of the operation — and you have worked out how many months of pension that comes to.

And perhaps you are already sitting in a corridor outside a consulting room where somebody like me is going to explain to you that there is no alternative.

There is an alternative. I say that as a man who insisted for thirty years that there was none.

Do not wait until the cartilage is gone completely. Do not wait until the trapped nerve dies — the disc can still come back, the nerve cannot. That I know for certain, it is my own field, and it is the only thing in my speciality that has not changed.

Do not wait until your children begin to talk among themselves about an old people's home.

Under this text there is a blue button — 'Learn more'. That is where Baba Adewale's official article is: the composition, how it works, accounts from people who got back on their feet. Read it carefully.

The courier delivers in three to four days, you pay on receipt, anywhere in Nigeria.

Send this on to your mother. To your father. To your husband. To your wife. To anybody who gets up in the morning groaning and tells you that everything is fine.

I have done more than three thousand eight hundred operations.

And the most correct of them all turned out to be the one I cancelled nine hours before the anaesthetic.

With respect,
Dr Obiora Ezenwa, neurosurgeon, 58, Lagos
