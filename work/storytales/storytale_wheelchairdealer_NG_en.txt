For twenty years I have been selling wheelchairs. Last year, for the first time, four of them were returned to me — almost new, with the receipts.

I did not send them back to the warehouse. Till today they are standing in my store, arranged in a row, covered with plastic. I cannot bring myself to sell them. Every time I open that door I look at them and think the same thing.

My name is Femi Adeniyi. I am fifty-two years old. I have a shop for rehabilitation equipment in Lagos — wheelchairs, walking frames, crutches, orthopaedic mattresses, bathroom lifts. Twenty years in this line of business. I know every supplier in this country by name.

And there is one thing I know about my customers that nobody will tell you out loud.

A wheelchair is goods you sell once.

Not because it lasts forever. But because the person who bought it never comes back. He comes back later for a bedsore cushion. Then for a bedsore mattress. Then his daughter comes and asks whether we accept second-hand equipment.

In twenty years I have sold over two thousand wheelchairs. And not one of them ever came back to me because it was no longer needed.

Until last year.

The first one was brought in February. Grace Okafor, seventy-one years, from Awka. We sold her the chair in May, two years before — I remember it, because her son took the most expensive one, with the light frame, and he priced it for a long time.

In February he called and said: “We want to return the wheelchair.”

I assumed his mother had passed on. That is the commonest conversation in my work and I know how it goes. I said: “Accept my condolences.”

He answered: “No, no. Mama is walking. She told me to remove that thing from the house, and I cannot throw away an item of three hundred and eighty thousand naira.”

I noted it down. I dropped the call. And for half a minute I sat there staring at the wall.

The second one was brought in April. Danladi Audu, sixty-six years, from Bukuru in Plateau State. A former tin miner. His children contributed together and bought him the chair, I remember the invoice.

He came alone. Behind the wheel. He came down from the car, opened the boot and lifted out the folded wheelchair with one hand.

I stood at the door of my shop and watched a man who two years before could not even sit into that chair by himself, now carrying it like a travelling bag.

He said: “I do not need it. Give it to somebody who needs it more.”

I asked: “What did you do?”

He looked at me in a strange way. He kept quiet for a moment. Then he said: “I went to see a man. Outside Lagos. An old papa, he deals with herbs.”

And that was all. He said nothing more. He entered the car and drove off.

The third one was brought in July. Comfort Bassey, seventy-eight years, from Uyo. Her daughter-in-law brought it. The chair was almost new — bought in January, used for six months.

The daughter-in-law dropped the receipt on the counter and said: “My mother-in-law ordered us to return it. She says if it remains in the house she will sit inside it again, and she does not want that.”

I asked her the same thing: “And what helped?”

She said: “She was taking something. Some kind of capsules. Her neighbour brought them for her. She says it is from one old man outside Lagos who heals with herbs.”

The second time. The same old man.

The fourth one was brought in October. Obinna Eze, fifty-nine years, from Onitsha. Still young by the standard of our trade — herniated disc, pressed nerve, one and a half years in the chair.

He also came alone. And he was the only one who spoke first.

He said: “Listen, you are the one selling this thing. You are supposed to know. Why is it that nobody tells us about this?”

I asked: “About what?”

He said: “That a wheelchair is not a sentence. My orthopaedic doctor told me: ‘Get used to it, from now on it will only get worse.’ And I got used to it. One and a half years I spent getting used to it. And then my cousin gave me a small container of capsules and the address of a village outside Lagos.”

I stood behind my own counter, the counter I have stood behind for twenty years, and I kept quiet.

Four wheelchairs. Four people from four different states. Not one of them knows the other. And all four of them named the same place.

That night I did not sleep. I opened the sales record of twenty years and started calculating.

Two thousand one hundred and forty-eight wheelchairs. The average “service life” — I know that figure offhand, because I need it to plan my orders.

Three years and four months. That is how long it takes on average from the sale of the chair to the moment the family stops buying consumables from me.

Three years and four months.

For twenty years I built my business on that figure. And only that night, at four in the morning, with my eyes fixed on that table, did I understand for the first time what exactly I had been counting for twenty years.

The following week I travelled to that village.

I will not name the village. I will only say that it is less than one hour from Lagos, then another twenty minutes on a bad road that washes away in the rainy season.

The house is wooden. A compound. A shed under which herbs are drying — in bundles, hanging with the heads down, dozens of bundles. A smell that hits you right from the gate.

Baba Adewale.

He is a hundred and two years old.

I did not believe it when they told me. I do not believe in anything at all — for twenty years I have been selling equipment to people whom everything else has already failed. My doubt is a professional matter.

He came out to me himself. He opened the gate himself. He was walking without a stick.

I trade in walking sticks. I know how a man of seventy walks, of eighty, of ninety. From the way a person walks I can tell what he will need in six months' time — a walking frame or a wheelchair. That is my job.

He was walking like a man of sixty.

We sat down in the compound. He asked me who I was. I told him the truth: “I sell wheelchairs. Four of them were returned to me. And all four people named you.”

He laughed. Quietly, without any triumph at all.

He said: “So I am doing bad business for you.”

I said: “Bad business.”

The two of us kept quiet.

Then he asked: “And what about your own back?”

I had not told him anything about my back. I do not tell anybody at all about it.

For twenty years I have been offloading wheelchairs. One chair — from fourteen to twenty-three kilos. The trailer comes once in two weeks. Thirty, forty pieces, and I do not keep a loader, because a loader is another salary.

My back started paining me at forty-three. By about forty-eight I stopped offloading in the evening — only in the morning, before it “locks”. Last year, for the first time in my life, I could not lift my own orthopaedic cushion from the floor. The very cushion I sell for eighteen thousand naira while telling my customers how much it helps.

For twenty years I have been trading in the health of people's backs. And I hide it from my wife that I sleep on my side with a pillow between my knees, because otherwise I cannot sleep at all.

He knew none of this. He simply asked.

I said: “The waist. For a long time now.”

He said: “Show me how you sit down.”

I sat down on the bench. He watched. Then he said: “You sit down on the right side. You are sparing the left. So that is where it is.” And he pointed his finger at the very place that not one of the four orthopaedic doctors I had been to ever hit with a finger.

He entered the house and brought out a small container. White, no label. Only writing by hand.

He said: “Take one capsule morning and night. Ten days. If it does not help — forget that you ever came. But give me ten days.”

I collected it. Not because I believed. But because I felt shy to leave with empty hands after the man had given me one full hour.

The first week, nothing.

That is to say — not completely nothing. On the fourth day in the morning I did not reach out my hand for support while getting up from the bed. I only noticed it in the evening, while going over the day in my head.

On the seventh I offloaded half a trailer and I did not sit down on a crate to catch my breath.

On the tenth day I bent down and tied my shoelaces. Standing upright. With both hands. Not through the knee, not on a stool, not sideways.

I stood in the store of my own shop, between the cartons of walking frames, and my hands were shaking. I am fifty-two years old. For four years I have been tying my shoes sitting down.

I called him that same evening.

He listened to me and said: “Good. Continue for two months. After that you will not need it again.”

I asked: “Baba Adewale. Do you understand what you are doing to my business?”

He said: “I understand. And do you understand what you are doing to yours?”

I could not find anything to answer.

Then he told me about his son. Tunde — he is not a doctor, he grew up among those herbs and weighing scales, he has known the recipe since he was a child. For years he was convincing his father that the formula should not remain in one village. He found a laboratory. He found suppliers of herbs. He started the production, so that the container could reach the person at home, instead of the person having to travel to the village.

Baba Adewale said: “I had a waiting list of six months. The people were not surviving the wait. In the end I agreed.”

I returned to Lagos and did not open the shop for three days.

I sat down and calculated. Not turnover — I was counting people.

Two thousand one hundred and forty-eight wheelchairs in twenty years. If even one out of every ten of them need not have sat inside it at all — that is two hundred and fifteen people.

Two hundred and fifteen people for whom I personally, with my own hands, carried out of the shop the very thing they afterwards sat inside forever. With a smile. With warranty. With a full consultation on choosing the model.

One month later the representative of my main supplier came to me. We have been working together for fifteen years.

He said: “Femi, what is happening with your orders? You have dropped by thirty per cent.”

I told him honestly: “I have stopped convincing people. Now I first ask whether they have tried everything.”

He looked at me and said a sentence which I wrote down word for word that same evening, because I was afraid nobody would believe me later.

He said: “Femi, our business does not grow from people who get well.”

I asked: “From whom does it grow then?”

He answered: “From those who have accepted their condition. Your work is not to disturb them while they are accepting it.”

He offered to raise my bonus to eighteen per cent if I returned to my previous volumes. Eighteen instead of eleven. That is about eight million naira a year.

I said: “No.”

He gathered his documents, stood up and said at the door: “We will find somebody else. There are plenty of them.”

And that is the most honest thing I have heard in twenty years in this line of business.

Doctors come to me. Not as doctors — as customers.

In November an orthopaedic doctor came. From a good Lagos teaching hospital, I have known him for about ten years, he sends patients to me.

He was buying a wheelchair for his mother. Seventy-nine years, osteoarthritis, the hip.

I asked him directly: “Doctor, and why do you not operate on her? You do operate.”

He was choosing a model without raising his head. Then he said: “Because I know how such operations end at seventy-nine.”

I put the catalogue away. I gave him the address of the village.

He wrote it down. He did not argue. He did not say one word about quackery, placebo or the mind playing tricks — and I had prepared myself to hear all three.

In February he passed by again. He did not collect the wheelchair.

And now the reason I am writing all of this in the first place.

My mother. Iyabo. Seventy-six years old. She lives in Ilorin, alone, in a house with a high flight of steps.

For three years she was telling me on the phone that with her “everything is fine, my son”.

For three years I had a shop in Lagos full of equipment that could have helped her. I brought her walking frames — she did not touch them. I brought her bathroom rails — she did not allow anybody to fix them.

Last rainy season I went without warning and met her sitting on the steps of the entrance. She had climbed up to the third step and sat down to catch her breath. She did not know how long she had been sitting like that.

For twenty years I have been selling equipment for people like my mother. And I could not make my own mother use it, because for her it meant admitting that everything was finished.

In June I brought her a small container. Not a wheelchair, not a walking frame, not rails. A container.

The first week I gave her the capsules myself, morning and night, because otherwise she would forget. After that she started taking them herself.

In September she called me in the evening and said that she had gone to the cemetery to my father. On foot. That is two kilometres from the house, and it is uphill all the way.

I listened to her and kept quiet on the phone, because I could not talk.

I am not a doctor. I am not a herbalist. I am not a scientist. I am a wheelchair seller whose year went wrong.

I do not know how that formula works at the cell level. They explained it to me — something about nanoparticles that pass through the stomach untouched, release only in the small intestine and travel through the blood straight into the joint, to places creams and ointments never reach. I have not verified it. I do not have a laboratory.

But I have a store. And inside it stand four wheelchairs, covered with plastic.

That is my laboratory. I do not have another one.

If you are reading this, it means that you, or your parents, have pain in the knees, the hips, the back.

Maybe they have already told you: “At your age that is normal.” Maybe they have already prescribed you a cream from the pharmacy that kills the pain for four hours. Maybe they have already told you the price of the operation — and you have calculated how many pensions it comes to.

And maybe you are already standing in my shop, choosing a model.

I want you to know this: the man behind the counter will not talk you out of it. He has a month to close. They hand him a target. He collects his eleven per cent, and if he makes an effort — eighteen.

For twenty years I was that man.

Do not wait until the cartilage is completely destroyed. Do not wait until the pressed nerve dies — the disc can still come back, the nerve cannot again; that is what they explained to me in that village outside Lagos and it is the only thing I memorised word for word.

Do not wait until your children start discussing an old people's home among themselves.

Forward this to your mother. To your father. To your husband. To your wife. To everybody who gets up in the morning with a groan and tells you that everything is fine.

I have sold two thousand one hundred and forty-eight wheelchairs.

I would like every one of them to be returned to me.

Yours sincerely,
Femi Adeniyi, 52 years, Lagos
